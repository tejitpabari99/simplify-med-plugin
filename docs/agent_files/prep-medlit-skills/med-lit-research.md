# `med-lit` research recommendation

Scope: design research for a separate skill that assesses the demands a health document places on readers. This is a recommendation, not an implemented feature or a validated instrument. The evidence below comes only from the actual contents of the specified Drive files and local repository files. Links found *inside* those files were not opened, so claims in internal research notes are attributed to the notes unless supported directly by one of the supplied PDFs. No web search or external facts were used.

## Decision in brief

Call the product a **health-material clarity and actionability review**, with a separately labeled **reading-level estimate**. Given one patient-facing document, it should identify concrete passages that may make the document harder to read, understand, navigate, or act on; explain why each passage was flagged; and offer source-preserving edits for a human to review. It must not claim to measure a person's health literacy, comprehension, ability to follow care, medical correctness, or clinical safety. AHRQ's toolkit separates readability from understandability and actionability, warns that a low formula score does not establish understanding, and recommends feedback from diverse patients. The supplied systematic review likewise separates individual ability from the accessibility of materials and systems and describes limited evidence that AI improves measured health-literacy outcomes. [E1, E2]

The existing `simplify-med` implementation already computes a rough Flesch–Kincaid (FK) grade and explicitly disclaims clarity, quality, and medical accuracy. `futures.md` proposes a standalone calculator using that same method, while the earlier project brief chose one non-gating before/after score for the simplification run. `med-lit` can reuse the formula, but should preserve its modest label and add a document audit rather than promote FK into a composite “health literacy” score. [E3, E4, E5]

## User promise, input, and output

**Promise:** “Show me where this document may be hard to read or act on, and what to review.” The target user is a document author, reviewer, or a patient examining a document; the result is a material-level audit and does not grade the patient. This product framing is a design inference from the material-focused AHRQ Tool #11 and the individual/material distinction in the review. [E1, E2]

**Input:** one English patient-facing document or extracted text, with optional title, document type, intended audience, source-page/section anchors, and whether it is a raw clinical note, a patient instruction, a lab-result display, or a simplified report. Ask for the original file when layout, tables, graphics, or reading order matter. Identify OCR/extraction gaps before assessing. The genre field matters because the supplied readability notes report that similarly graded EHR notes and general health information can be perceived differently; the AHRQ toolkit also treats visual design and format as part of material usability. [E1, E6]

**Output:** a short summary of supported observations; FK estimate with word count and an explicit “rough estimate” caveat; a dimension-by-dimension checklist with `observed / not observed / not assessable`, the exact excerpt and location for every finding, a suggested edit or reviewer question, and a confidence/coverage note. Keep a machine-readable result if useful, but make the human report usable without a single total score. For documents under the existing 30-word threshold, return `insufficient text for FK`, not zero or a fabricated grade. For text-only inputs, mark visual accessibility, charts, and layout `not assessable`. These output rules are design recommendations grounded in the code's behavior and AHRQ's multi-method assessment advice. [E1, E3]

## What to measure

| Dimension | V1 observation | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface reading load | FK grade, sample size, long sentences/paragraphs; report separately and never gate the whole assessment. | FK uses counted words, sentences, and heuristic syllables; it does not measure understanding. [E1, E3] |
| Jargon and abbreviations | Flag undefined acronyms, technical words, and dense clusters; ask whether an essential term is retained and explained. | A short abbreviation can make a formula look easier while increasing reader difficulty. Do not automatically replace a precise medical term. [E6, E7] |
| Meaning and organization | Check whether purpose, key point, section headings, sequence, and essential context are visible; flag unclear referents, multiple ideas in a sentence, and unexplained relationships. | These are qualitative observations, not an inferred comprehension score. The supplied criteria prioritize plain words, one idea per sentence, organized sections, and a short summary. [E7] |
| Actionability | For an instruction actually present, check whether the actor, action, timing, and applicable condition are explicit; separate “no action appears” from “a required action is missing.” | An assessor cannot invent a care action or decide that one is clinically required. AHRQ treats actionability separately from understandability. [E1, E7] |
| Numeracy and result display | Flag unlabeled values, units/ranges without explanation, calculations left to the reader, and a chart without nearby verbal context. Preserve exact numbers and source labels. | Do not infer normality, urgency, risk, prognosis, or a patient-specific target from a value. The project criteria themselves warn that added labels may become medical interpretation. [E7, E8] |
| Presentation and access | If rendered material is available, inspect heading hierarchy, crowding, whitespace, table/chart labels, visual reading order, and whether meaning depends on color alone. | A plain-text pass cannot establish visual or assistive-technology accessibility. AHRQ includes design and format in its material review. [E1] |
| Source fidelity | For a proposed edit, compare its quantities, negations, uncertainty, actors, and actions with the original excerpt. | A fluency improvement that changes medical meaning is a failure. The technical POC treats unchanged meaning as paramount; the repository separates scoring from fidelity checks. [E9, E10] |

Do not compute an overall number from these dimensions in V1. The checklist has mixed evidence types and missing-by-design categories; a total would imply a weighting and validation that the supplied evidence does not establish. The research notes describe alternative medical-specific, semantic, and numeracy measures, but those are leads for later validation, not ready-made transferable thresholds. [E1, E6]

## Deterministic and model-assisted components

**Deterministic:** reuse `fk_grade` unchanged initially; report its 30-word minimum and heuristic nature. Count candidate acronyms, numeric expressions, sentence/paragraph lengths, and explicit headings with transparent parsing rules; retain exact spans and offsets. Run structural checks only on representations where the structure survived extraction. These are reproducible indicators, not diagnoses of difficulty. The existing tests cover formula output, the 30-word boundary, syllable heuristics, and CLI behavior, but not medical abbreviation or layout edge cases. [E3, E11]

**LLM-assisted:** review the flagged spans and surrounding context for undefined terminology, unclear meaning, missing explanation of a *stated* action, overly implicit numeracy, and edit suggestions. Require a quoted span/location plus a short rationale for every finding; allow `uncertain` and `not assessable`; keep the original technical term where dropping it could lose meaning. Never let the model silently “correct” clinical content. The Technical POC proposes pairing word lists with an LLM and warns against changed meaning or fabricated specificity; its proposal is not evidence that the method is already validated. [E9]

**Human review:** clinical-content changes, perceived clarity, accessibility, and usefulness need review with actual readers or qualified reviewers. AHRQ advises involving patients, obtaining diverse feedback, and combining methods; the systematic review found little participant engagement in the AI literature it surveyed. [E1, E2]

## Uncertainty, fairness, and failure behavior

Use “may be hard to follow” and show the evidence span; do not call a person low literacy. A single score should not rank people or be used to personalize to presumed ability. The Health Literacy Review summarizes personal literacy as finding, understanding, appraising, and applying information and notes that formal individual tools involve separate administration; the supplied systematic review explicitly distinguishes individual ability from material accessibility. [E2, E12]

Disclose English-only scope for V1. Do not apply English FK to other languages or treat a translation as assessed merely because its English source scored well. Do not use the presence of medical terminology, a language variety, disability-related format, or a user's request for simpler wording as evidence about that individual's competence. Recruit and report performance for diverse readers, including differences in language needs and access modes. AHRQ Tool #11 calls for languages readers understand and alternatives to print; Tool #17 warns that convenience and portal-only samples can be biased. The supplied notes also describe differences in perceived accessibility for nonnative English readers. [E1, E6]

If extraction is damaged, the language is unsupported, the document is too short, or the genre/visual information needed for a dimension is absent, return a partial assessment with the affected dimensions marked `not assessable`. If an action or numeric flag could require clinical interpretation, phrase it as a reviewer question and avoid a “fix” that adds content. If the model cannot cite an actual source span, omit the finding or label it unverified. If no source-preserving edit is possible, say so. These are proposed safety rules inferred from the source-fidelity priorities and the limited meaning of formula scores. [E1, E3, E7, E9]

## Minimal V1, later work, and alternatives

**V1:** English text or well-extracted PDF/Doc text; one document; FK as a separate rough estimate; structured qualitative audit of terminology, organization, stated actions, and numeracy; excerpt-level evidence; no overall score, rewrite-on-save, medical advice, or individualized literacy profile. Include a visible extraction/coverage note. A design with separate observations fits AHRQ's combined assessment approach and the existing repository's narrow FK utility. [E1, E3]

**Later, after evidence:** rendered-layout and screen-reader review; dedicated result-display rubric; multilingual methods validated per language; reader-selectable detail levels; reader studies; calibrated medical concept familiarity; and, only if validated against the intended use, a weighted or comparative score. The repository's `futures.md` explicitly defers reading-level presets and a separate calculator; the research notes describe medical concept, semantic, and numeric-density alternatives but do not establish a production threshold for this plugin. [E4, E6]

**Alternatives considered:** (1) FK-only calculator: simplest and consistent with `futures.md`, but misses jargon, meaning, and actions. (2) Full PEMAT score: a useful model for separate understandability/actionability domains, but requires an item-by-item implementation, applicability rules, and validation for the specific document genres; do not call a loose LLM checklist “PEMAT.” (3) Learned medical-readability ranker: promising in the supplied notes, but needs representative labeled material and reader judgments, and is a larger validation project. (4) One opaque LLM “health-literacy score”: easiest to display but least auditable and most likely to be mistaken for an individual or outcome measure. [E1, E3, E6, E9]

## Validation and kill test

Before launch, build a small, consented/appropriately handled corpus spanning patient instructions, after-visit summaries, raw notes, lab displays, short documents, acronym-heavy lists, and extraction failures. Have independent reviewers annotate exact issue spans and permitted source-preserving edits. Test deterministic counts against hand-calculated fixtures and edge cases (short text, abbreviations, bullets, tables, decimal numbers, ranges, repeated headers, OCR noise). Test model finding precision/recall and span support, inter-reviewer agreement, and edit fidelity, especially quantities, negation, uncertainty, timing, and urgency. Compare the audit to FK-only and assess whether readers can find and explain the key action in structured tasks; include diverse readers and access modes. Report uncertainty and subgroup coverage rather than pooling away weak performance. This plan follows AHRQ's advice to combine approaches and get diverse patient feedback; it is a proposed validation protocol, not a claim of measured efficacy. [E1, E2, E3, E11]

**Kill test:** give the skill an acronym-heavy clinical note with short sentences, a low FK estimate, ambiguous numeric values, and an explicit instruction whose negation/timing is easy to alter. It must flag the relevant jargon and ambiguity with exact spans, decline to assign individual literacy or clinical urgency, and preserve the action and numbers in any suggested edit. Also give it a 20-word document and a scanned page with lost layout: it must withhold FK for the short text and mark the damaged visual dimensions unassessable. Failure on these cases blocks release. The construction targets the known formula and code limitations and the source-fidelity boundary. [E3, E6, E7, E9, E11]

## Open decisions

1. Which genres are in V1: only materials written for patients, or raw clinician notes and lab reports too? Genre affects the benchmark and which checklist items are applicable. [E1, E6]
2. Is `med-lit` read-only, or may it propose a revised version? If it revises, what existing fidelity checker or human approval covers the suggestion? [E9, E10]
3. Should the result preserve only excerpt/section locations or page coordinates as well? That depends on extraction quality and intended review workflow. [E1]
4. Who is the primary user of the audit: a patient, document author, or clinical reviewer? This determines tone, technical detail, and actionability expectations. [E1, E7]
5. What empirical acceptance thresholds will be set for span support, false positives, edit fidelity, reader comprehension, and subgroup coverage? The supplied sources do not establish thresholds for this skill. [E1, E2]
